FOR VETERANS IN CARE AND THEIR FAMILIES

Agent Orange, the PACT Act and toxic exposure

Many veterans in nursing homes served in the Vietnam era. VA now presumes many of their illnesses were caused by service, which can mean new benefits for the veteran or the surviving spouse.

Why this matters in nursing homes

Many veterans now in long-term care served in the Vietnam era. Several serious illnesses, including Parkinson's disease, ischemic heart disease, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and some cancers, are on VA's Agent Orange list. When a condition is “presumptive,” the veteran does not have to prove service caused it. They only need to meet the service requirement.

A veteran who has never filed, or was denied before, may now qualify for disability compensation. A surviving spouse may qualify for Dependency and Indemnity Compensation if the veteran died of one of these conditions.

Agent Orange: conditions VA presumes

Cancers: bladder cancer; chronic B-cell leukemia; Hodgkin's disease; multiple myeloma; non-Hodgkin's lymphoma; prostate cancer; respiratory cancers, including lung cancer; some soft tissue sarcomas.

Other illnesses: AL amyloidosis; chloracne; type 2 diabetes; high blood pressure (hypertension); hypothyroidism; ischemic heart disease; monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS); Parkinsonism; Parkinson's disease; early-onset peripheral neuropathy; porphyria cutanea tarda. (Chloracne, early-onset peripheral neuropathy and porphyria cutanea tarda must have been at least 10% disabling within a year of exposure.)

High blood pressure and MGUS were added by the PACT Act. A condition not on the list can still be claimed with more evidence.

Where VA presumes exposure

  • The Republic of Vietnam, any length of time, from January 9, 1962, to May 7, 1975, including aboard a U.S. military vessel in the inland waterways, or on a vessel no more than 12 nautical miles offshore of Vietnam and Cambodia (“Blue Water Navy”).
  • Any U.S. or Royal Thai military base in Thailand, January 9, 1962, to June 30, 1976.
  • Laos, December 1, 1965, to September 30, 1969.
  • Cambodia at Mimot or Krek, Kampong Cham Province, April 16 to April 30, 1969.
  • Guam or American Samoa or their territorial waters, January 9, 1962, to July 31, 1980.
  • Johnston Atoll, or a ship that called there, January 1, 1972, to September 30, 1977.
  • In or near the Korean Demilitarized Zone, September 1, 1967, to August 31, 1971.
  • Certain Air Force units with C-123 aircraft, and anyone who transported, tested or stored Agent Orange.

Denied before?

A Blue Water Navy veteran denied in the past can file a Supplemental Claim based on the change in law. Anyone denied for a condition VA now presumes can also file a Supplemental Claim. A survivor can submit a new DIC application.

The PACT Act, burn pits and Gulf War service

The PACT Act expanded VA health care and benefits for veterans exposed to burn pits, Agent Orange and other toxic substances. It added more than 20 presumptive conditions, including many cancers and breathing conditions, for veterans who served in the Gulf War or after September 11, 2001, in the countries VA lists, and added radiation cleanup and response efforts to VA's radiation list. Veterans who served in the Vietnam War, the Gulf War, Iraq, Afghanistan or another post-9/11 combat zone, or who were exposed to toxins in service, can enroll in VA health care without first applying for disability benefits.

PACT Act questions: 800-698-2411.

The toxic exposure screening

Every veteran enrolled in VA health care gets an initial toxic exposure screening and a follow-up at least once every 5 years. It asks about burn pits and other airborne hazards, Gulf War exposures, Agent Orange, radiation, Camp Lejeune water and other exposures, then points to benefits, registry exams and care. Ask for it at the next VA appointment, or ask the VA medical center for the toxic exposure screening navigator.

VA also offers a free Agent Orange Registry health exam for veterans who meet the service requirements. It is not a claim exam and is not needed to get benefits.

How to file

  • Gather the DD214 or other separation papers showing where and when the veteran served, and a medical record showing the diagnosis. The facility's doctor or medical records office can provide the diagnosis.
  • Submit an intent to file to protect the start date while you gather records.
  • Get free help from an accredited representative: your county veterans office or VA’s representative search. See who may charge fees.

What to do next

If you are the veteran

If you served in Vietnam, Thailand, Korea near the DMZ or on a ship offshore, and you have one of the conditions above, ask your county veterans office to file for you.

If you are the spouse

If your spouse died of a condition on this list, you may qualify for DIC, even if VA denied a claim before.

If you are an adult son or daughter

Ask the facility for a current list of diagnoses and compare it with the list above. Find the DD214 or ask the county veterans office to request it.

If you are a social worker or facility staff

Ask veterans about service dates and places at admission. A resident with Parkinson’s disease, ischemic heart disease or diabetes who served in Vietnam is worth a referral to the county veterans office.
