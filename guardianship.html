FOR VETERANS IN CARE AND THEIR FAMILIES

Guardianship and the choices to try first

When a veteran or older adult can no longer handle money or medical choices, families often hear “get guardianship.” Many people need less than that.

Start with the least restrictive choice

A court guardianship takes decisions away from a person, often for the rest of their life. Ohio law requires a probate court to consider evidence of a less restrictive alternative before appointing a guardian. Many families can handle money and medical choices without going to court, especially if papers are signed while the person can still understand them.

Papers to sign while the person can

Financial power of attorney

Names an agent to handle money and property. In Ohio, the law is Revised Code Chapter 1337, and section 1337.60 has a standard form with a checklist of powers. An Ohio power of attorney is presumed to be durable, which means it keeps working if the person later loses capacity, unless it says otherwise. It can start right away or only when something happens, such as incapacity.

Know the limits: the agent must act in the person's best interest, but there are few safeguards against misuse, and banks or nursing homes sometimes refuse a power of attorney, especially one that only starts on incapacity. A power of attorney covering real estate must be recorded with the county recorder.

Health care power of attorney and living will

A health care power of attorney names someone to make medical decisions when the person cannot. A living will states wishes about life-sustaining treatment. In Ohio, a health care power of attorney can be revoked at any time and in any manner.

For veterans who get care from VA, VA has its own form: VA Form 10-0137, VA Advance Directive: Durable Power of Attorney for Health Care and Living Will. It tells VA providers who may make decisions and what care the veteran wants.

Supported decision-making

The person keeps the right to decide, and chooses trusted people to help them understand options and communicate. Releases of information, direct deposit and automatic bill paying can support this. The Ohio Developmental Disabilities Council's guide, Supported Decision Making and Guardianship, explains the options.

Other tools

Ohio also has trusts, an additional signer on a bank account, and conservatorship, a court arrangement a person who is still competent can choose for themselves. Adding someone as a joint owner of an account is risky: a joint owner has no duty to the person, and the change can affect eligibility for government benefits.

VA benefits: the VA fiduciary

VA makes its own decision about whether a veteran or survivor can manage VA payments. If VA decides they cannot, it says so in the decision letter, waits 60 days so the person can ask for a hearing or send more evidence, and then refers the case to the VA Fiduciary Program.

  • VA considers the person's choice. A spouse, relative or trusted friend who serves without a fee can be the fiduciary if they meet VA's requirements; otherwise VA appoints a professional fiduciary.
  • A fiduciary manages VA benefits only. It does not take away the right to manage non-VA money, to vote, or to sign legal documents.
  • The person can ask for a decision review within 1 year of the letter, and can later ask VA to end the fiduciary when they can manage again.
  • VA Fiduciary program: 888-407-0144. Misuse: see how to report it.

When guardianship is needed: Ohio probate court

  • The probate court of the county where the person lives appoints guardians. A guardian can be named for the person (care and daily decisions), the estate (money and property), or both.
  • The court must hold a hearing. Incompetency must be proven by clear and convincing evidence, and evidence of a less restrictive alternative must be considered when it is offered.
  • The person has the right to their own lawyer, to have a friend or family member present, and to an independent expert evaluation. If they cannot afford a lawyer or evaluator, they can ask the court to appoint one at court expense.
  • The court can appoint a limited guardian with only the powers needed; the person keeps every right the order does not cover. In an emergency, the court can appoint an emergency guardian for up to 72 hours.
  • Guardians of adults must complete court education: a one-time six-hour fundamentals course and three hours of continuing education each following year. The Supreme Court of Ohio offers free courses.
  • When a guardianship is mainly to handle money awarded by VA, Ohio law says no court costs are charged unless the estate, including the VA award, is worth more than $1,500.

In other states, contact the probate or county court where the person lives. For free legal help for people 60 and older, ask the local Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator: 800-677-1116). In Ohio, people 60 and older can also call the Pro Seniors legal hotline: 800-488-6070.

What to do next

If you are the veteran

If you can still decide for yourself, sign a health care power of attorney and a financial power of attorney now, and tell your family where they are. Ask the social worker for VA Form 10-0137 if you use VA care.

If you are the spouse

Being married does not give you authority over your spouse’s accounts or medical care in every situation. Check what papers exist and whether the bank and the facility will accept them.

If you are an adult son or daughter

Before filing for guardianship, find out what your parent already signed. A limited guardianship, or no guardianship, may be enough.

If you are a social worker or facility staff

Keep copies of powers of attorney and advance directives in the chart. If an agent or guardian seems to be misusing money, report it to APS and the probate court.
