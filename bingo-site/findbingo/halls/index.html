Help for bingo halls

Free listings, help reaching players, and your state’s rules on bingo money and ads, in plain language.

Players come here to find a game. Halls and the groups that run them come here to be found. Every listing is free, and the help below is shaped to what your state allows.

What we offer every hall

  • Your listing is free, always. No hall can pay for a better one.
  • Help getting the word out. Free tips, and paid advertising on Find Bingo if you want more.
  • Featured events. Put a special bingo night in its own box for people searching near you. How featured events work
  • A game site for your hall. We build it, host it and keep it current from the updates you send us, for one flat monthly fee. You get a page to put on flyers, signs and social media. Details

Partnering with a charity, where your state’s rules allow

States treat bingo money in three different ways, and we only ask for what fits:

  • The law requires some groups to give to an outside charity (Indiana and Ohio). In Ohio, where Veteran Home Guardians works, a hall can name us as one of its recipients. We ask for a modest share of that required gift, usually 5 to 10 percent, not all of it. How partnership works in Ohio Elsewhere, we help you find a local partner charity first.
  • The law lets a group give to other causes (Minnesota, North Dakota, Virginia and West Virginia). If you want a partner cause, we help you find a local one first.
  • The money stays with your group (Florida, Kentucky, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, New Mexico, New York, Pennsylvania, Texas and Washington). We never ask for a share. We help you reach players instead.

Local partners, state by state

Outside Ohio we would rather bingo support stay close to home, with the groups already doing the work there. In these states we name a local partner and ask halls to consider supporting it, in whatever way their own rules allow. It is never a condition of being listed.

State rules we have checked

Plain-language summaries with their sources. This is not legal advice; your state regulator has the final word.

Florida

After prizes and the actual costs of the game, the proceeds must go to the charitable, civic, community, benevolent, religious or scholastic work of the group running it, and to nothing else. Homeowners’ and similar associations may donate what is left after prizes to a tax-exempt charitable, nonprofit or veterans’ group.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: Florida Statutes 849.0931 ↗

Indiana

Net proceeds may be used only for the group’s own lawful purposes. A group that gets at least 90 percent of all its gross receipts from charity gaming must donate 60 percent of its gaming receipts after prizes to a qualified recipient that is not its own affiliate, parent or subsidiary.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: Indiana Gaming Commission, Allowable Expenses ↗ · Ind. Code 4-32.3-5-4 ↗

Kentucky

Licensed groups must keep at least 40 percent of their adjusted gross receipts from charitable gaming and spend it on their own charitable purposes.

Ads: An ad for charitable gaming must carry the name of the group running it and its license number, and may not advertise a bingo prize above $5,000 in 24 hours.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: 2024 annual report ↗ · KRS 238.536 ↗ · KRS 238.540(9) ↗

Michigan

The entire net proceeds of a bingo event must go to the licensed group’s own lawful purposes.

Ads: Where the state lets a group advertise bingo, the ad must say what the net proceeds will be used for.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: MCL 432.109 ↗ · MCL 432.110 ↗

Minnesota

Gambling profits may be spent only on lawful purposes or allowable expenses that the membership approves. Each year a group must spend at least 30 percent of gross profits on lawful purposes (20 percent where the main business is bingo), and lawful purposes include charitable contributions.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: Minn. Stat. 349.15 ↗ · Gambling Control Board ↗

Mississippi

All net proceeds from bingo must be spent only on the purposes the charitable organization was created for.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: 13 Miss. Code R. 10-5.5 ↗

Missouri

The entire net receipts above the actual cost of the game must go to the lawful, charitable, religious or philanthropic purposes of the group running it.

Ads: Advertising each bingo occasion is an allowed expense at fair market cost, bought under the Gaming Commission’s rules.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: RSMo 313.040 ↗

New Mexico

The entire net proceeds of a game must go to the lawful purposes of the group that runs it.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: Gaming Control Board FAQ ↗ · NMSA 60-2B-3 as amended in 2007 ↗

New York

Net proceeds must go to the lawful purposes defined in the state’s bingo licensing law: the charitable, educational, religious, patriotic and similar causes the group was formed for.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: General Municipal Law 476 ↗ · General Municipal Law 475 ↗

North Dakota

Net proceeds must be paid out within the time set by rule, only for educational, charitable, patriotic, fraternal, religious or public-spirited uses, often as gifts to recipients that use them that way.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: N.D.C.C. 53-06.1-11.1 (Games of Chance Laws, effective August 1, 2025) ↗ · N.D. Admin. Code 99-01.3-14 ↗

Ohio

Veteran, fraternal and sporting groups must give at least 25 percent of their first $330,000 of instant bingo net profit each year, and at least 50 percent of anything above that, to an outside charity or a government unit. Other charities use all of it for their own purposes.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: Ohio Adm.Code 109:1-4-21 ↗

Pennsylvania

Money from bingo must support the nonprofit purposes of the association that holds the license; a county can revoke a license if it is used for anything else.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: 10 P.S. 306 ↗

Texas

A licensed group must put its net proceeds from bingo toward its own charitable purposes.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: Texas Occupations Code 2001.454 ↗

Virginia

Groups must use at least 10 percent of their gross receipts (20 percent of electronic gaming adjusted gross receipts) for their religious, charitable, community or educational purposes. Those payments may go to scholarship funds or to religious, charitable, educational or community causes.

Checked November 20, 2026. Sources: 11VAC20-20-20 ↗ · 11VAC20-20-110 ↗

Washington

Bingo must be run by the group itself, and no part of the proceeds may benefit anyone other than the group running the game. After the necessary costs of the games, all net income must go to the lawful purposes the group stated when it applied for its license. Running a game for the benefit of specific named recipients needs the Gambling Commission’s approval first.

Checked November 22, 2026. Sources: RCW 9.46.0205 ↗ · WAC 230-07-010 ↗

West Virginia

All net proceeds must be spent within a year of the license on the charitable or public service purposes named in the application, by giving them to a qualified recipient organization or, with the Tax Commissioner’s approval, on a charitable or public service activity the group sponsors itself.

Checked November 21, 2026. Sources: W. Va. Code 47-20-15 ↗ · W. Va. Code 47-20-2 ↗
