OTHER-THAN-HONORABLE, GENERAL AND BAD-CONDUCT DISCHARGES

PTSD, TBI, MST and mental health: liberal consideration

If what happened to you in service had a part in the conduct that got you discharged, the boards are required to look at your case with liberal consideration. Here is what that means and what evidence helps.

What liberal consideration means

When your request rests in whole or in part on a mental health condition, including PTSD and traumatic brain injury, or on sexual assault or sexual harassment, the board is required to review it with liberal consideration (Air Force boards, citing the Kurta memo). In plain terms: the board gives you the benefit of the doubt that the condition or experience existed and played a part, then weighs it against what you did.

For PTSD and TBI tied to combat or military sexual trauma, it is also the law. Both kinds of board must review those claims with liberal consideration that the condition potentially contributed to the discharge, and they must use mental health professionals in the review (10 U.S.C. 1553(d), 10 U.S.C. 1552(g) and (h)). Discharge Review Boards must expedite those cases too.

The four memos, in order

Hagel, September 3, 2014

Secretary of Defense guidance to the Boards for Correction on PTSD (memo):

  • Liberal consideration for service treatment record entries that document symptoms of PTSD or related conditions.
  • Special consideration for VA decisions that document PTSD connected to service.
  • Liberal consideration for civilian diagnoses when the record supports symptoms at the time of service.
  • Time limits to reconsider decisions are to be liberally waived.
  • The other side: boards weigh mitigation carefully when the misconduct was serious, and the memo says PTSD is not a likely cause of premeditated misconduct.

Carson, February 24, 2016

The Boards for Correction will waive the statute of limitations for these PTSD and TBI cases. Cases decided earlier without this guidance get a fresh review on request (memo). If you were turned down before 2014, that matters.

Kurta, August 25, 2017

Clarifying guidance to both kinds of board for requests based on mental health conditions, sexual assault or sexual harassment (memo). It is the memo boards cite for what liberal consideration means and the four questions below.

Wilkie, July 25, 2018

Standards for relief on equity, injustice or clemency, whether or not a mental health condition is involved (memo). More below.

The four questions a board asks

Board decisions applying the Kurta memo work through these, in this order. You can read real, redacted decisions in the DoD boards reading room to see how your board answers them.

  1. Did you have a condition or experience that may excuse or mitigate the discharge?
  2. Did that condition exist, or that experience happen, during military service?
  3. Does the condition or experience actually excuse or mitigate the discharge?
  4. Does the condition or experience outweigh the discharge?

Build your application so each answer is easy to find: what the condition or experience was, when it started, how it connects to the incidents, and why it outweighs them.

Evidence that counts, even without a diagnosis

The Air Force boards list relevant evidence as including, but not limited to (Air Force boards):

  • A diagnosis of PTSD, TBI or another behavioral health condition from a medical professional, made in service or after.
  • Records from service or after that document traumatic experiences in service.
  • Records from a medical professional or licensed social worker noting symptoms, even if no formal diagnosis was made.
  • Letters from people who knew you before, during or after service describing changes in behavior or symptoms.

The Navy’s review board adds that you can submit signed, sworn statements from family, peers, doctors, counselors or people you served with saying you showed signs or symptoms of a mental health condition during service (NDRB).

Where to look in your own record: a drop in evaluations or conduct after a deployment, injury or assault; sick call visits; counseling statements; the dates of the incidents compared with the dates of what happened to you. A timeline that lines these up does a lot of work.

Want a diagnosis or an evaluation? VA emergency mental health care, a Vet Center or a community provider can help (care you can get now). Some free legal programs require a diagnosis before they take a mental health case (free help).

Fairness and clemency: the Wilkie memo

Even without a mental health condition, a board can grant relief to make the outcome fair. The memo tells boards, among other things (memo):

  • Military custom favors second chances for people who have paid for their misdeeds.
  • Relief is not only for high achievers. Character and rehabilitation weigh more than achievement alone.
  • An honorable discharge does not require flawless service.
  • Evidence can come from outside your service record, and your own sworn testimony, oral or written, may establish a fact.
  • If you would likely get a better outcome under today’s policies, that can be grounds for relief. Some misconduct is viewed as less severe today than when it happened.
  • Unfair differences in punishment between similar cases can be grounds for relief.
  • Relief is generally more appropriate for nonviolent offenses than violent ones.

Boards also consider your candor, whether the punishment was too harsh, what you have done since (arrests, jobs, school, family, community), acceptance of responsibility, time since the misconduct, character references and letters of recommendation. The Air Force correction board asks for an FBI Identity History Summary with an upgrade request, because post-service conduct weighs heavily (Air Force boards).

Turned down before? What “new evidence” means

A denial is not always the end. The rules for asking again:

  • Discharge Review Boards reconsider on new, substantial, relevant evidence that was not available at the original review. They also reconsider if you had only a record review and now want a hearing, if you now have a lawyer or representative and did not before, or if a policy change was made retroactive (32 CFR 70.8).
  • The Board for Correction of Naval Records reconsiders on new and material evidence or other matter not previously considered. New means the board did not see it and you could not reasonably have gotten it before. Material means it is likely to substantially affect the outcome (32 CFR 723.9). The other correction boards have similar rules; check your board’s page.
  • Decided before the PTSD guidance? Cases decided without the 2014 guidance get a fresh review on request (Carson memo).

Things that often qualify as new: a diagnosis you did not have before, a VA decision connecting a condition to service, statements from people who were not asked the first time, and records you did not have.
