OTHER-THAN-HONORABLE, GENERAL AND BAD-CONDUCT DISCHARGES

What VA can do without an upgrade

VA does not simply go by the box on your DD-214. For other-than-honorable and most bad-conduct discharges, VA looks at your service and decides for itself. Some care is open to you right now.

Rough night? Call 988 and press 1, or text 838255. A VA medical center will see you in an emergency whatever your discharge.

VA decides for itself

An honorable or general (under honorable conditions) discharge meets VA’s discharge requirement for most benefits. For undesirable, other-than-honorable and bad-conduct discharges, VA reviews the record and makes its own decision, called a character of discharge determination (VA).

  • VA’s decision is for VA benefits and services only. It does not change your discharge.
  • A rule that took effect June 25, 2024 removed one bar entirely and created a “compelling circumstances” exception for two others. VA says people it turned down before may now be eligible and should apply again (VA, final rule).
  • In April 2024, VA said that over the previous 10 years it had provided care or benefits to 75% of the former service members with these discharges who went through this review (VA announcement).

VA’s own health care page lists two ways to qualify with bad paper: apply for a discharge upgrade, and ask VA for a character of discharge review (VA). Do both. They run separately.

How to ask

You start the review by applying for a benefit. VA then looks at your service before it decides the claim.

  1. Apply for VA health care, and for disability compensation if you have conditions from service. A free accredited service officer can file with you (free help).
  2. Include a written statement about your service and what led to the discharge. Be honest about the misconduct and explain what was going on.
  3. Send evidence of the things VA weighs under the compelling circumstances exception (38 CFR 3.12(e)):
    • The length and quality of your service apart from the misconduct: deployments, awards, evaluations, promotions.
    • Mental or cognitive impairment at the time. The rule names PTSD, depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorder, ADHD, impulsive behavior and cognitive disabilities, and it counts evidence that could later be medically shown to prove one, not only a diagnosis made at the time.
    • Physical health, including physical trauma and side effects of medication.
    • Combat-related or overseas-related hardship.
    • Sexual abuse or assault.
    • Duress, coercion or desperation, and family obligations.
    • Your age, education, cultural background and maturity at the time.

Care you can get now

Emergency mental health care

If you are in crisis, go to the nearest VA medical center. VA says it does not matter what your discharge status is or whether you are enrolled. If a provider finds you are at risk of immediate self-harm, VA may provide or pay for emergency mental health care and up to 90 days of related services if at least one of these is true (VA):

  • You were the victim of sexual assault, battery or harassment while serving.
  • You served on active duty for more than 24 months and did not get a dishonorable discharge.
  • You served more than 100 days under a combat exclusion or in support of a contingency operation, including as a Reservist, and did not get a dishonorable discharge.

At a non-VA emergency room, tell the staff you are a veteran and ask them to contact VA right away.

Care for conditions from service

Under 38 CFR 3.360, VA health care is provided to certain former service members with administrative other-than-honorable discharges for disabilities incurred or made worse in the line of duty. It does not apply after a bad-conduct discharge or when one of the statutory bars below applies.

Vet Centers

Vet Centers offer readjustment counseling based on how you served. You qualify if you served in a combat theater or area of hostility (VA lists Iraq and Afghanistan) or experienced military sexual trauma, among other routes. VA says it looks for reasons to qualify people rather than turn them away (VA). Call 877-927-8387 to find the nearest one.

The bars to benefits

Some discharges block VA benefits no matter what VA thinks of the rest of your service. There are two kinds (38 CFR 3.12).

Statutory bars (set by law)

  • Conscientious objector who refused to perform military duty, wear the uniform or obey lawful orders.
  • Discharge by sentence of a general court-martial.
  • Officer resignation for the good of the service.
  • Deserter.
  • Alien who asked for release during a period of hostilities.
  • Other-than-honorable discharge issued because of AWOL for 180 continuous days or more. The compelling circumstances exception can apply to this one.

Regulatory bars (set by VA)

  • Taking an other-than-honorable discharge in lieu of trial by general court-martial. No exception.
  • Mutiny or spying. No exception.
  • An offense involving moral turpitude, which generally means a felony conviction. The compelling circumstances exception can apply.
  • Willful and persistent misconduct. The compelling circumstances exception can apply. Under the rule, minor incidents within two years of each other count as persistent, as does a minor incident within two years of more serious misconduct, or serious incidents within five years of each other.

An insanity exception covers every bar, using VA’s legal definition of insanity, which is not the everyday meaning. The 2024 rule also removed the old bar for “homosexual acts” (VA).

Which upgrade clears a bar at VA

An honorable or general discharge from a Board for Correction is final for VA and sets aside any bar. An upgrade from a Discharge Review Board after October 8, 1977 sets aside the regulatory bars but not the statutory ones (38 CFR 3.12(f) and (h)). If a statutory bar applies to you, such as 180 days AWOL, plan on the Board for Correction (boards).

Benefits outside the character of discharge review

A general discharge meets the discharge requirement for most VA benefits, but some ask for honorable service. For example, two of the Post-9/11 GI Bill’s eligibility paths require an honorable discharge (VA). That is one reason veterans with a general discharge still apply for an upgrade. State benefits have their own rules; ask your state veterans agency.
