OTHER-THAN-HONORABLE, GENERAL AND BAD-CONDUCT DISCHARGES

Bad paper is not the end of the road

Other than honorable, general or bad conduct on your DD-214? You have likely been told you get nothing. That is often wrong. Here is what is still open, how an upgrade works, and where to start this week.

Rough night? Call 988 and press 1, or text 838255. A VA medical center will see you in an emergency whatever your discharge.

The short version

  • VA makes its own call. An other-than-honorable or bad-conduct discharge does not automatically shut you out. When you apply, VA reviews your service and decides whether it counts for VA purposes. This is the character of discharge determination. It does not change your discharge. In 2024 VA added a “compelling circumstances” exception and asked people it had turned down before to apply again (VA).
  • Some care is open now. In a mental health emergency, go to a VA medical center. VA says your discharge status does not matter for that, and VA may cover emergency mental health care and up to 90 days of related services if you meet its service rules (VA).
  • The discharge itself can be changed. A Discharge Review Board can review a discharge within 15 years. After that, or for a general court-martial, the Board for Correction of your branch takes the case (which board, which form).
  • PTSD, TBI, military sexual trauma and other mental health conditions count. Boards must give those cases “liberal consideration” (what that means).
  • Help is free. County veterans offices, accredited service officers, legal aid and pro bono lawyers do this work at no cost. Do not pay anyone up front (free help).

You can do two things at the same time: ask VA to decide on your service now, and ask a board to upgrade the discharge. VA lists both as ways to qualify (VA health care eligibility). One does not wait on the other.

Where to start this week

THIS WEEK

  1. Read your DD-214

    Find the character of service (block 24) and the reason and codes (blocks 25 to 28), and note your separation date. Those tell you which board you use and whether the 15-year window is still open. No DD-214? Order it now: get your records.

  2. Order your full personnel file and medical records

    Ask for the whole file, not only the DD-214. The paperwork behind your discharge is in there, and requests take time. The National Archives does this free for veterans (how).

  3. Apply to VA anyway

    Apply for VA health care, and for disability compensation if you have conditions from service. VA will review your service. Add a short written statement about what was going on when things went wrong (what to include).

  4. Call one free helper

    Your county veterans service office, an accredited service officer, legal aid or a law school clinic. Ask: “Do you help with discharge upgrades and VA character of discharge requests?” (who to call).

  5. If you are struggling, do not wait on paperwork

    VA emergency mental health care does not wait for any of this. Call 988 and press 1, or walk into a VA medical center.

THE NEXT FEW WEEKS

  1. Write your timeline

    Dates of deployments, injuries, blasts or head hits, assaults, losses, when you started having trouble, and the incidents that led to the discharge. Plain words are fine. This becomes your statement to VA and the board.

  2. Line up statements

    People who knew you before, during and after service can describe the changes they saw. Boards accept these, and they count even without a diagnosis (evidence that counts).

  3. Pick your board and form

    Discharged less than 15 years ago: Discharge Review Board, DD Form 293. More than 15 years, or a general court-martial: Board for Correction, DD Form 149. VA’s online questions give you the right form and mailing address (details).

If you are helping someone

Family members and friends carry a lot of this. What helps most:

  • Do the legwork, let them sign. You can make the calls, track the requests and keep copies. The veteran signs the applications. The Navy’s review board, for one, will not accept a representative’s signature unless the veteran has died or is legally incompetent, with proof (NDRB).
  • Write your own statement. If you knew them before and after, describe what changed: sleep, drinking, temper, pulling away, memory. Boards weigh letters from people who saw the change (evidence).
  • Keep one folder and one log. What was sent, to whom, on what date, and any case or docket number. Send copies, never originals.
  • Let them set the pace. Some of what goes in these applications is hard to put on paper, especially military sexual trauma. A free attorney or service officer can help them say it once, in the right place.
  • Watch for crisis signs. A denial letter or digging up old records can hit hard. 988, then press 1, works for family members too.
